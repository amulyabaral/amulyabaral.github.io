Antibiotic use and antibiotic resistance in Norwegian cattle

Amulya BaralSupervisors: Ingrid Toftaker · Ann-Katrin Llarena · Yngvild Wasteson

Welcome. One sentence on the thesis, then zoom out to the system this work sits in.

Antibiotic resistance is a One Health problem

  • 1.1 million deaths a year.
  • 73% of antibiotics go to food animals.

Naghavi et al. Lancet 2024 (deaths attributable to resistance, 2021 and forecast) · Van Boeckel et al. Science 2017

The problem in two clicks. Before the first: resistant bacteria are everywhere. Under antibiotics the susceptible ones die and the resistant ones survive and multiply (Davies and Davies 2010). (1) GRAM 2024 (Naghavi et al., Lancet): 1.14 million deaths attributable to and 4.71 million associated with bacterial resistance in 2021; forecast for 2050: 1.91 million attributable, 8.22 million associated. (2) About 73% of all antimicrobials sold are used in food animals (Van Boeckel et al. 2017), close to 100,000 tonnes in 2020 (Mulchandani et al. 2023). Use per kg of animal fell 5% worldwide in 2020–2022 and 23% in Europe (WOAH 2025).

One Health: resistant bacteria and their genes circulate between people, animals and the environment through food, contact, manure, water and soil. In high-use systems manure holds up to 28,000 times more resistance genes than background soil or water (Zhu et al. 2013; He et al. 2020).

Norway is at the other end of the scale. (1) Farms and cattle, Statistics Norway, 1 March 2025 (Register of Production Subsidies; tables 03688 and 03710, and 03312 for all holdings): 11,686 holdings with cattle (6,108 with dairy cows, 5,760 with beef cows; some keep both) out of 36,439 agricultural holdings. 844,004 cattle: 208,064 dairy cows, 105,109 beef cows and 530,831 other cattle (heifers, calves, bulls and steers). Herd size: 72 cattle per holding with cattle (844,004 / 11,686), 34 dairy cows per dairy holding, 18 beef cows per beef-cow holding. Statistics Norway's own table 05985 (animals per holding, final 2025 figures) gives 73.7, 34.8 and 18.8 on a slightly different holding basis, against 34.6, 14.4 and 7.3 in 2000: herds have doubled in 25 years while the number of cattle holdings halved. Provisional figures for 1 March 2026: 851,218 cattle on 11,443 holdings (74 per holding); 204,342 dairy cows on 5,975 holdings; 105,105 beef cows.

(2) The 9% (NORM/NORM-VET 2023, page 28, Table 6 and Figure 16; Blix, Grave and Hansen): sales, not use, by weight of active substance, from the Norwegian drug wholesales statistics database of the Norwegian Institute of Public Health plus the wholesale data for fish farming reported to the institute; each product is placed in a sector by its ATC or ATCvet code (humans: A01, A07, D06, D07, D09, D10, R01AX, G01, J01, P01AB, S01–S03; animals: QA07, QD06, QD07, QG51, QJ01, QJ51, QS01–QS03; farmed fish: QJ01). In 2023, 58.4 tonnes in all, 16.4 of them methenamine (read from the figure); the shares are of the 42 tonnes without methenamine: humans 90.6%, terrestrial animals 8.1% (food-producing animals, horses and companion animals together), farmed fish 1.2%: 9.3% for animals, about 3.9 tonnes. With methenamine counted, the animal share would be about 6.7%. Later reports do not repeat this split. The veterinary chapter counts 4,479 kg for terrestrial animals in 2023 (372 kg of it for companion animals) and 548 kg for farmed fish, so the two chapters do not count identically; without dogs and cats, food-producing animals and fish take about 8.6%. Globally about 73% of antimicrobials sold are used in animals raised for food (Van Boeckel et al. 2017). Sales for terrestrial animals are the lowest since recording began in 1993: 4,479 kg in 2023, 4,341 kg in 2024 and 4,272 kg in 2025; use is among the lowest in Europe (EMA 2025).

(3) Cattle are the main users among the four species whose use must be reported to EMA (cattle, pigs, turkeys and chickens). VetReg 2024, NORM/NORM-VET 2025 Figure 10, read from the bars: cattle about 1,267 kg, pigs 532, turkeys 61, chickens 5, of about 1,865 kg: 68%, 29% and 3%. In 2025: cattle 1,305, pigs 415, turkeys 20 and chickens 8 kg: 75%, 24% and 2%. Coverage against sales in 2024: 80% for cattle and pigs, 109% for poultry. Not in the split: sheep, goats, horses, farmed fish and companion animals, whose use is not reported by species. NORM-VET reports sales for "food-producing terrestrial animals, including horses" as one group (3,984 kg in 2024; 3,905 kg in 2025, of which 26% was trimethoprim-sulfa paste for horses), companion animals (357 kg in 2024) and farmed fish (709 kg in 2024, 498 kg in 2025). The ESUAvet report for 2024 lists Norway at 1.3 tonnes for cattle and 0.50 tonnes for pigs in 2024 (1.3 and 0.88 tonnes in 2023); NORM-VET 2025 notes that its own 2024 figures differ after methodology updates and removal of double reporting. The species bar is drawn as a blow-up of the terrestrial-animal part of Norway's bar, but holds only these four species.

(4) Cattle only, not humans or all animals: indicator E. coli from caecal samples of cattle taken at slaughter throughout 2023 by the Norwegian Food Safety Authority, one animal per herd (EU harmonised monitoring, Implementing Decision (EU) 2020/1729; NORM-VET does not restrict it to cattle under one year). E. coli grew from 271 of 277 samples. 93.7% of the 271 isolates were susceptible to every antimicrobial class in the panel; 4.4% were resistant to one class, 1.1% to two and 0.8% to three or more (6.3% in all: the six burgundy dots); sulfamethoxazole resistance was the most frequent; two isolates (0.7%) were resistant to extended-spectrum cephalosporins through chromosomal ampC mutations, none to meropenem. The share has been stable since 2015 (NORM/NORM-VET 2023, pages 66–69 and 174). Resistance to critically important antimicrobials is rare to absent. Antibiotics are prescription-only, and every treatment of terrestrial food-producing animals has had to be reported to VetReg since 2012: the source of the species figures. Objectives

  2. Develop a systematic approach to error correction
  3. Estimate use with mass-based and dose-based indicators

Regulation (EU) 2019/6 · EMA 2025 (ESUAvet) · Norway 2009 (FOR-2009-07-03-971) · Grave et al. 2020 · NORM/NORM-VET 2025 · Udhwani et al. 2025 BMC Vet Res · Norström et al. 2026 Prev Vet Med

The background, in two clicks, then the three objectives, one per click. (1) Use is counted: Regulation (EU) 2019/6 made reporting of antimicrobial use by species mandatory, starting with the 2023 data for cattle, pigs, chickens and turkeys (other food-producing species from 2026; dogs, cats and fur animals from 2029). EMA publishes them in the ESUAvet reports, the first in March 2025 with the 2023 data; Norway reports on the same terms through the EEA agreement, with VetReg as its source. These are national totals per species and animal category, with variable coverage and accuracy (EMA 2025): no indication or herd. Before that, NORM-VET quantified use from wholesaler and feed-mill sales, which carry neither species nor indication. Norwegian stewardship has moved from broad reductions to prudent use and targeted refinements (One Health strategy 2024), which needs use by antimicrobial, indication and route. (2) VetReg has received all prescriptions for terrestrial food-producing animals since 2012, with product, indication and route, so years of history are there; but data quality has limited its use for quantitative reporting, particularly for intramammaries. (3–5) The objectives, as in the manuscript: (1) assess the data quality on antimicrobial use for cattle in VetReg, (2) develop and implement a systematic approach for error correction, (3) estimate use in Norwegian cattle with mass-based (kg active substance) and dose-based (DDDvet, DCDvet) indicators. The question behind them, in the words of the thesis aims: can historical VetReg prescription data be validated to estimate antimicrobial use in Norwegian cattle by class, indication and route?

Detail: reporting to VetReg is mandatory under FOR-2009-07-03-971 for veterinarians, pharmacies and feed mills. Grave et al. 2020 documented substantial discrepancies between VetReg and wholesaler sales, particularly for intramammary antimicrobials in cattle, with underreporting by veterinarians and pharmacies as contributing factors. NORM-VET has therefore expressed use in cattle only as proportions of total use. VetReg captured 85% of the wholesaler-reported volumes in 2023 (Udhwani et al. 2025) and about 78% in 2024, so EMA uses Norway's cattle data for progress tracking only (ESUAvet). Norström et al. 2026 gave the first DDDvet estimates for 2016–2021 but took intramammary use from wholesaler sales. Status of the paper: under review in Preventive Veterinary Medicine (Elsevier; manuscript PREVET-D-26-00971), revision submitted.

Clicks: the register, the cattle records, the three quality attributes, the two corrections, the metrics.

Detail: VetReg data for 2018–2023 from the Norwegian Food Safety Authority: 12.6 million entries, of which 4.4 million for cattle; 698,864 records with antimicrobial ATC codes (688,193 veterinarian-administered treatments, 10,671 pharmacy sales). Product information from the SPC via FEST (DigiVet deliverable 2.3). Quality attributes after Birkegård et al. 2018: completeness as the share of missing values in five key variables; validity as agreement of the recorded unit with the SPC packaging unit and as plausibility of the recorded quantity; timeliness as days from dispensing to registration. Plausibility thresholds per product: from the minimum therapeutic dose for a 40 kg calf to twice the maximum licensed course for a 1,300 kg bull; for fixed-unit formulations from the treatment duration and the number of anatomical sites; reviewed by two cattle specialists. Corrections: units harmonised to the SPC unit; quantities outside the thresholds replaced by the product-specific mean therapeutic dose; number of animals treated recoded to one where a treatment was split over several records. Quantities are converted to active substance with the SPC strength per package unit or, where the strength is stated per applicator or vial, per applicator or vial, and salts, esters and prodrugs to the active moiety (revision 1 corrected this step for six intramammary presentations). Metrics: kg active substance and mg/kg biomass (Statistics Norway table 03710, 425 kg per animal); nDDDvet per kg biomass; intramammary and intrauterine products as nDDDvet or nDCDvet per 1,000 cows (EMA 2016).

If use is this low, what does the resistome look like?

EMA 2025 (ESUAvet 2024) · Udhwani et al. 2025 BMC Vet Res · Grave et al. 2020 · Norström et al. 2026 Prev Vet Med

The main finding, its context and the main limitation. Bridge: the driver is small and well characterised; the next question is the reservoir. The next click zooms out over the system and into Paper II.

Detail: to our knowledge the first complete prescription-level characterisation of antimicrobial use in Norwegian cattle, with every category, intramammaries included, quantified from one source. ESUAvet 2024: only eight countries (22.9% of EU cattle biomass) met the coverage threshold; 229 tonnes, about 28 mg/kg cattle biomass, mostly tetracyclines and penicillins; no quantitative cattle estimate for 2023. Our 3.4 mg/kg in 2023 is roughly eight-fold lower, and narrower in spectrum (87.9% beta-lactamase-sensitive penicillins). VetReg coverage against wholesaler data was about 70% in 2016, 85% in 2023 and about 78% in 2024, and could not be estimated for cattle alone, so the totals are lower bounds and the decline is likely conservative. The recoding of ambiguous records as single-animal treatments and the replacement of implausible quantities (about 5.5% of veterinarian records) act in the same direction; a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S4) moves the annual totals by at most 2.3%. Systemic use agrees with Norström et al. 2026 (0.3 DDDvet per animal and year; ours 0.24–0.27, 9–21% lower after cleaning); intramammary use 1.4 per dairy cow-year from sales against 0.65 per cow-year from prescriptions (0.96 per dairy cow; 1.24 after scaling by the 78% coverage). Not on the card, for questions: the paper recommends a reporting platform with a single unit per pharmaceutical form, the active-substance mass computed from the SPC, and a feedback report to each prescriber as in the Danish VetStat (Jensen et al. 2004). The thresholds are tied to the products on the market in 2018–2023; the pipeline can be re-run on later years. Implication: granular surveillance from a single prescription-based source, in support of targeted stewardship.

Paper II · manuscript

Defining a baseline resistome: fecal antimicrobial resistance genes in Norwegian cattle under minimal antibiotic selection

HUNT One Health, Trøndelag, 2018–2019

BackgroundResistance surveillance is culture-based. The resistome is uncharacterised.
Objectives
  1. Characterise the faecal resistome and microbiome
  2. Quantify the drivers: antibiotic use, farm, season, age and sex
  3. Compare with public cattle faecal metagenomes
  4. Determine the genomic context of ARGs, to assess mobility

NORM/NORM-VET 2023

The background, then the four objectives, one per click. (1) Resistance surveillance has relied on culturing indicator organisms and clinically relevant pathogens (over 90% of commensal E. coli from cattle fully susceptible; penicillin resistance in S. aureus from mastitis about 5%). It shows a favourable picture, but the metagenomic resistome, with the genes carried by unculturable and commensal bacteria and their mobility, was uncharacterised. A low-use baseline is not necessarily a low-resistance one: ARG diversity persists in systems with little current exposure (Munk et al. 2018; Lawther et al. 2022). (2–5) The objectives: the three aims of the manuscript draft (characterise the faecal resistome and microbiome of Norwegian cattle; describe how resistome composition varies with recent antibiotic use, farm, season, age and sex; determine the genomic context of the detected ARGs to assess their mobility potential) and, from its methods, placing the Norwegian resistome in a global context with public cattle faecal metagenomes run through the same pipeline; listed in the order of the talk. The question behind them, in the words of the thesis aims: what is the faecal resistome of Norwegian cattle, and is it shaped more by farm-level factors or by recent antimicrobial treatment? A low-use baseline against which high-use systems can be read.

Detail. Cohort: 673 sequenced libraries; 547 cattle faecal samples from 185 farms once 60 extraction blanks, 62 mock communities and 4 samples from other animals (3 dogs, 1 horse) were set aside. 31 had fewer than 15 million host-removed read pairs, leaving 516 (184 farms). Six with 2% or more Streptomycetaceae (4–86% of the community; post-collection overgrowth, farm-specific Streptomyces species) were removed: 510 animals on 183 farms. Both rules were applied before any resistance gene was counted. Sampling: owners smeared fresh faeces on dried blood spot cards, up to three animals per farm, April 2018 to May 2019; age, sex and antibiotic treatment in the past 12 months were recorded. Sequencing: QIAsymphony PowerFecal Pro extraction with a blank and a mock community on every plate; ThruPLEX libraries; NovaSeq 6000, 150 bp paired-end, about 20 million read pairs targeted; fastp, host reads removed against ARS-UCD1.2 with Bowtie2. Taxa profiling: SingleM single-copy marker genes, depth of coverage per lineage in genome copies; families in at least 10% of animals; the summed bacterial genome equivalents are the denominator of ARG abundance. AMR profiling, two routes. Assembly and genomic context: MEGAHIT per sample (per farm as a check); ARGs on contigs with ABRicate against ResFinder (≥ 90% identity, ≥ 80% coverage); geNomad for plasmid-like contigs, Bakta for mobility genes within 5 kb, CAT on the ARG-masked contig for the host family. Read-based: KMA against MEGARes v3 (over 90% identity and 80% coverage), point-mutation genes removed, the Drugs domain only; 74 gene groups in 14 classes after removing 12 intrinsic E. coli genes. Statistics (not on the card): burden = ARG copies per 100 bacterial genome equivalents; PERMANOVA (farm unrestricted; age, treatment and aerobic share within farm); within-farm models for treatment, mixed models for age; LinDA as a compositional check. Global context (not on the card): public cattle faecal metagenomes from the ENA (healthy animals, faecal samples, no individually documented treatment, at least three samples per study), technical runs merged and the same 15 M filter on host-removed pairs: 209 samples from 14 studies (USA, Canada, Brazil, Australia, China and one European study), run through the same pipeline and compared within life stage.

Feeding trial at the NMBU animal research centre; same sequencing and ARG scope as Paper II

BackgroundThe diet is changing: the methane inhibitor 3-NOP. Its effect on the resistome is unknown.
Objectives
  1. Estimate the effect of 3-NOP and diet on ARG load
  2. Test for a shift in resistome composition

EFSA 2021 / EU authorisation 2022

The background, then the three objectives, one per click, from the analysis plan. (1) A changing diet: 3-nitrooxypropanol (3-NOP, Bovaer) blocks the last step of methane formation in the rumen; its effect on the faecal resistome is unknown. (2) Primary: ARG load, log(ARG depth / bacterial depth), roughly ARGs per bacterium, in a linear mixed model adjusted for the baseline sample: 3-NOP × diet × month, cow as random effect. (3) Whole-resistome composition: Aitchison distance (Euclidean distance between CLR profiles), PERMANOVA month by month, and the distance from each cow's own baseline. (4) Exploratory: which genes and taxa changed, trusting those flagged by both LinDA and ANCOM-BC2. The question behind them: does 3-NOP change the load or the composition of the faecal resistome?

Bovaer is being rolled out for the climate. Before it is everywhere, check that it does not shift gut bacteria or the resistance genes they carry.

Status: the trial and the sampling are done and the samples have been sent for sequencing; nothing has been analysed yet.

3-NOP blocks the last step of methane formation

  • 3-NOP blocks the last enzyme of methane formation.
  • About 30% less methane in dairy cows.
  • Does it also shift the bacteria and their resistance genes?

Duin et al. 2016 PNAS; Kebreab et al. 2023 J Dairy Sci (meta-analysis); EFSA 2021 / EU authorisation 2022

Mechanism in one line, then the open question. 3-NOP is not an antimicrobial, but a shift in the community can change which bacteria, and which ARG carriers, dominate.

Detail: 3-NOP inactivates methyl-coenzyme M reductase, the enzyme of the last step of methane formation in rumen archaea (Duin et al. 2016). In dairy cows it lowers methane production by about 30% at the recommended dose (Kebreab et al. 2023). Authorised in the EU in 2022; now entering Norwegian herds. The hydrogen that no longer becomes methane goes elsewhere, so fermentation shifts; diet alone also shifts gut bacteria.

Roadmap for integrated One Health AMR surveillance in Nordic countries

Anna Abramova, Amulya Baral, Adriana Dorota Osińska, … Patrick Munk, Veronika Kuchařová Pettersen · Public Health 2026;255:106285

BackgroundAll five Nordic countries have excellent systems for tracking AMU and AMR.

But the granularity is lost during reporting.

Objectives
  1. Map the surveillance systems and datasets
  2. Identify the obstacles to integration
  3. Propose a roadmap to integrate them

European Commission 2018 (One Health Action Plan against AMR) · Davido et al. 2025 Lancet Reg Health Eur · Council Recommendation 2023/C 220/01 · NordForsk NoMoReAMR

Zoom out. The system we just looked at sits in one corner of a region with the same problem and five separate ways of watching it. Published in Public Health (review article, open access), 17 authors from the five countries, from the NordForsk network NoMoReAMR.

The background in two clicks, then the three objectives, one per click. (1–2) The Nordic countries were early adopters of AMR and antimicrobial use surveillance (DANMAP 1995, FINRES and Finres-Vet, the Icelandic programme, NORM/NORM-VET, Swedres-Svarm), but each country has its own framework. Data are shared mainly through EU and international platforms of lower resolution than the national datasets; human, animal, food and environmental surveillance run as separate streams with different granularity and timeliness; delayed reporting means no early warning across borders. (3–5) The objectives, in the words of the published abstract: map the existing surveillance systems and research assets (compiled on nomoreamr.org); identify the key challenges to cross-border alignment (five, each with a proposed solution, Table 2); and propose practical steps toward coordinated cross-sector, cross-country surveillance. The question behind them, in the words of the thesis aims: which antimicrobial use and resistance data do the Nordic countries hold, and how can they be integrated?

Five mature systems, run country by country

Mapped from public reports, surveillance publications and institutional documents into one open resource:

nomoreamr.org
  • Five national programmes; DANMAP the first, in 1995.
  • Humans, animals and food in each; the environment only in pilots.
  • Across borders mainly through EU and WHO platforms, at lower resolution.

Abramova et al. 2026 Public Health (Table 1, Figures 1 and 2) · Directive (EU) 2024/3019

How the paper was made, then what it found, one click each. Mapping and review: national surveillance programmes, data infrastructures and institutional actors, collected from publicly available reports, surveillance publications and institutional documentation and compiled into nomoreamr.org; the authors also assessed the technical, legal and ethical requirements for linking the national systems. The report grew out of a three-day meeting at DTU, Denmark. (1) Five national programmes: DANMAP (Denmark, since 1995), FINRES and FINRES-Vet (Finland), the Icelandic annual report on AMU and AMR (first in 2012), NORM and NORM-VET (Norway; NORM since 1999), Swedres-Svarm (Sweden; Swedres since 2002). Technically mature and increasingly molecular (Denmark sequences many pathogens routinely), yet underused as a regional resource. (2) Each covers humans (H), animals (A) and food (F); environmental surveillance (E, dashed) is pilot or research-based only: as of 2025 there is no systematic environmental AMR surveillance in Europe, and the recast Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive 2024/3019 now makes AMR surveillance of urban wastewater mandatory. (3) Cross-border exchange goes mainly through European and international platforms (EARS-Net, ESAC-Net, ESUAvet, the EFSA/ECDC EU Summary Report, WHO GLASS), which often lack the resolution of the national systems. The region's density of public genomic sequences is over seven times the global average.

Abramova et al. 2026 Public Health (Table 2)

Table 2 of the paper, one obstacle per click, each with its proposed solution and what it would bring. (1) Many systems report only summaries or aggregated results, which prevents reanalysis: systems that give access to raw data, for flexible analysis, transparency and integration across datasets and countries. Access to individual-level data is constrained by the GDPR and fragmented approvals, so a regulatory framework and a secure joint platform are needed; national summaries plus detailed datasets are a pragmatic start. (2) Susceptibility results reported only as S, I or R, without the quantitative data: store raw MIC values and disk zone diameters, so that results can be reclassified when EUCAST breakpoints change. (3) Laboratory results not connected to patient records (diagnosis, history, outcome): a secure link between laboratory and clinical data that respects privacy, building on electronic medical records, WHO GLASS-compatible formats and unique patient identifiers (pilots: EHDS@LV, HealthData@EU). (4) Data reported annually or quarterly, which delays outbreak detection: near-real-time sharing among laboratories, hospitals and public health authorities, in practice a notification system for selected pathogen–drug combinations, building on the national notification systems. (5) Data mostly from hospital patients or diagnostic submissions: sample healthy and sick individuals across the human, animal and environmental sectors, and monitor sewage as a composite sample of a whole city.

Decentralised collection, centralised insight

  • Decentralised collection: each country keeps its system.
  • Centralised insight: one secure platform; federated analysis where data cannot leave.
  • Earlier detection, joint stewardship, more statistical power.
  • Feasible, with structured linkage and clear legal and ethical rules.

A model for other regions

Abramova et al. 2026 Public Health

The model the paper proposes, built up. (1) Decentralised collection: each country keeps its own national system. (2) Centralised insight: standardised, de-identified data shared on one secure Nordic platform, with federated machine learning for joint analysis of data that cannot be shared directly. (3) What it gives: earlier detection of emerging resistance, more consistent, evidence-based policy and coordinated antimicrobial stewardship, less duplication, complementary coverage across countries and sectors, and more statistical power for research. Comparability by design: coordinated sampling and harmonised susceptibility testing across sectors, with joint training. (4) The conclusion: feasible, but it requires structured linkage of the existing national systems and clear ethical and legal frameworks for data access and sharing; the mapping resource supports these technical and governance steps. (5) A template for other regions.
