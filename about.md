### What is my PhD about?

**In short: Antibiotic use and resistance in Norwegian cattle.**

About 80% of all antibiotics produced in the world is actually used in animals. We've known for a while that increasing antibiotic use in animals eventually comes around to affecting humans by making infections (in both animals and humans) difficult + costly to treat and sometimes even deadly because it selects for antibiotic resistant bacteria. Norway is an interesting place to study this because there's very little use of antibiotics here and there are excellent registries that keep very good track of it all. BUT, resistance genes still show up (although not as much as other places), so I want to know where they come from, why they stick around, whether they can move between bacteria, and how the Nordic countries could keep better track of the whole problem. It has four parts:

#### 1. How much antibiotics do Norwegian cattle actually get?

You can't reduce antibiotic use if you don't know how much is being used in the first place. Norway has a national register of vet prescriptions, but the raw data has been (really really) messy — wrong units, impossible amounts, etc. I took 1 million prescription records from 2018–2023, built a systematic way to clean that data, and put together the first complete estimate of antibiotic use in Norwegian cattle.

#### 2. Which resistance genes live in cattle guts, and can they move?

As I said earlier, Norwegian cattle get hardly any antibiotics, yet resistance genes still show up in the bacteria in their feces. If the genes survive without antibiotic pressure, simply using less antibiotics won't make them disappear. And some genes sit on pieces of DNA that can jump between bacteria, so they could spread to pathogenic bateria. I used metagneomic sequencing data from 500+ samples to figure out which resistance genes there are, whether they sit on mobile bits of DNA, and what actually explains the variation in the genes that we see across different samples..things like the farm, the animal's age, or a recent antibiotic treatment. I then downloaded all publicly available metagenomes that were similar to my samples and compared my samples against them (and showed how different they are).

#### 3. Does a climate-friendly feed additive change gut bacteria?

A feed additive called Bovaer makes cattle burp out (and fart) less methane, so it's being rolled out to help slow down climate change. Before it ends up everywhere, it's worth checking that it doesn't also shake up gut bacteria or the resistance genes they carry. We have a feeding trial with 56 cattle where half got the additive and half didn't, and are following their gut bacteria and resistance genes over two months to see if anything changes.

#### 4. How could the Nordic countries track resistance together?

Each Nordic country tracks resistance and antibiotic use in its own way, and humans, animals, food and the environment are usually tracked separately. That makes it hard to compare countries or spot a problem early, before it crosses a border. I was very lucky to be a part of the NoMoreAMR project. We mapped every surveillance system in the Nordics into one open website [nomoreamr.org](https://nomoreamr.org) and wrote a roadmap for tying them together. It's doable, but it needs connected data systems and clear rules for sharing data across borders. [Paper published in Public Health, 2026.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.puhe.2026.106285)

### Wear the same clothes everyday
